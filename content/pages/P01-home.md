## What to Do in the First 30 Minutes

1. **Stop the water if you safely can.** Most King County homes have a main shutoff where the water line enters the house, often in the crawl space, garage, or near the water heater, plus a valve at the meter box near the street.
2. **Stay away from electricity.** If water is near outlets, cords, or the electrical panel, don't walk through it. Turn off power at the breaker only if you can reach it from a dry spot.
3. **Keep out of sewage.** If the water came from a toilet, floor drain, or sewer line, keep children and pets away and don't try to mop it up.
4. **Take photos and video** of the water, the source, and damaged belongings before anything is moved. Your insurance company will ask for them.
5. **Move what you can.** Lift rugs, electronics, documents, and photos off wet floors. Put foil or plastic under furniture legs.
6. **Call for help and call your insurer.** Most homeowners policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, so don't wait for an adjuster to visit before drying starts.

## Why Water Damage Is So Common in King County

- **Long, wet winters.** From October through April, back-to-back storms and atmospheric rivers soak the ground. Once soil is saturated, groundwater finds its way into crawl spaces and daylight basements.
- **River valleys and creeks.** The Snoqualmie, Cedar, Green, and White rivers, and creeks like Issaquah Creek and Bear Creek, run right past neighborhoods. During high water, nearby homes can see seepage and backups even when the river stays in its banks.
- **Windstorms and power outages.** A sump pump can't run without power. Outages during winter storms are one of the most common reasons basements and crawl spaces flood.
- **Cold snaps.** Hard freezes don't happen every year here, so many pipes in crawl spaces, exterior walls, garages, and foothill cabins aren't protected. When a freeze finally comes, pipes burst, and they often start leaking only when they thaw.
- **The houses themselves.** Many King County homes sit on crawl spaces, older homes still have aging galvanized supply lines, and newer two-story homes often have upstairs laundry rooms where a failed hose can soak two floors.

## A Quick Word on Insurance

Standard homeowners policies usually cover water damage that is **sudden and accidental**, such as a burst pipe or a failed appliance hose. They usually **do not** cover slow leaks that went on for weeks, rising floodwater from outside (that requires separate flood insurance), or sewer backups unless you have a water backup endorsement. Every policy is different, so read yours or call your agent. Our [insurance claims guide](/insurance-claims) walks through the process.

## How to Choose a Restoration Company

Whoever you hire, it's worth checking:

- **Washington L&I contractor registration.** Contractors working on homes in Washington must be registered with the Department of Labor & Industries. You can look them up on the L&I website.
- **IICRC training.** The IICRC S500 is the industry standard for water damage restoration. Ask whether the technicians on your job are IICRC-certified.
- **Written moisture readings.** A good company measures and records moisture every day until the job is dry.
- **A clear authorization form.** Read what you sign, especially anything that involves insurance payments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How fast can someone get to my home?**
It depends on your location, the time of day, and how many calls are coming in. During a regional storm, demand spikes. The company you're connected with will give you an arrival estimate on the phone.

**Is it really free to call?**
Yes. The referral costs you nothing. You only pay if you choose to hire a restoration company, and many sudden water losses are billed to homeowners insurance.

**Can I just dry it myself with fans?**
For a small spill on a hard floor that you caught right away, maybe. For anything that soaked carpet, drywall, a subfloor, or a crawl space, household fans won't pull the moisture out of the structure, and hidden moisture is what leads to mold and rot.
