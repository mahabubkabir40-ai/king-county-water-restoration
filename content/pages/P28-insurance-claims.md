## 1. Why You Shouldn't Wait for an Adjuster Before Stopping the Damage

Nearly every standard homeowners insurance policy includes a clause often called **"Duties After Loss"** or **"Duty to Mitigate."** In plain English, it means the homeowner is expected to take reasonable emergency steps to protect the property from further damage once a leak is discovered—such as shutting off the water and getting standing water extracted and dried.

If water is left sitting for a week while waiting for an adjuster to visit, the insurer may question secondary damage (such as mold or warped floors beyond the initial leak area) that happened because mitigation was delayed.

**The golden rule:** Document everything with clear photos and video *before* anything is moved, keep the broken plumbing part if a pipe or valve failed, and start water extraction right away.

## 2. What Standard Policies Usually Cover vs. What Usually Requires Extra Coverage

Always read your own policy declarations and exclusions, as coverage varies by carrier and policy form:

### Often Covered ("Sudden and Accidental" Internal Water Discharge)
- **Burst or frozen pipes** (provided the home wasn't left vacant without heat or water shut off)
- **Sudden appliance overflows or hose ruptures** (washing machines, dishwashers, refrigerator ice-maker lines)
- **Sudden water heater ruptures** (the resulting water damage to floors and walls is often covered, even though replacing the worn-out tank itself usually is not)
- **Sudden toilet or tub overflows** involving clean or gray water
- **Rain entering through a sudden storm-damaged opening** in the roof or wall (for example, when a falling branch punches a hole in the roof)

### Often Excluded unless You Have a Separate Endorsement or Policy
- **Slow, long-term leaks or seepage** that went on for weeks, months, or years
- **Rising outside floodwater or surface runoff** (such as river flooding in the Snoqualmie, Cedar, or Green River valleys, or overland mud/stormwater—this typically requires a separate **Flood Insurance** policy through the NFIP or a private flood insurer)
- **Groundwater seepage** through foundation walls or crawl space soil
- **Sewer, septic, or sump pump backups**—unless you added a **Water Backup and Sump Discharge Endorsement** to your homeowners policy
- **Cost to repair the broken pipe or appliance itself** (insurance usually pays for the resulting water damage to the house, not the plumber's bill to replace a worn valve)

## 3. Step-by-Step: How to Document Your Water Loss

1. **Take wide and close-up photos and video** of the standing water, the rooms affected, and any damaged belongings before cleanup starts.
2. **Photograph and save the failed part.** If a braided supply line, washing machine hose, pipe fitting, or valve burst, take a clear photo of it and ask your plumber to leave the broken piece with you. Adjusters often ask to see it.
3. **Keep receipts** for emergency plumber visits or supplies.
4. **Ask the restoration company for full drying documentation.** Reputable restoration companies record initial moisture mappings, thermal images, equipment counts, and daily moisture meter readings, and many prepare estimates in industry-standard software (such as Xactimate) that insurance adjusters use.
5. **Read any work authorization before signing.** Make sure you understand the scope of emergency mitigation, how the company bills your insurer, and what your deductible or out-of-pocket responsibility is if a claim is not covered.
